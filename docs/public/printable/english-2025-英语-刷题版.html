Dog Trainers Dog trainers teach dogs, especially puppies, through a structured process. Test Sleepers Test sleepers need to sleep in a hotel and test out everything about it. In the end, they need to write a report on it, such as its cleanliness, bed comfort, noise levels, price, etc. Their detailed feedback helps customers choose hotels and helps hotels improve services. They are tired sometimes because they go to different hotels every day. Earnings: Tasks pay 50-200 dollars per night, depending on the hotel's star rating.

These part-time jobs prove that with creativity and effort, anyone can turn hobbies or unique skills into extra cash. Whether you love animals, art, or adventures, there's a flexible job waiting to be explored.

Passage C

For the seventh year running, the Finns have taken the top spot as the world's happiest people. That's according to the World Happiness Report 2024 released on March 20. As usual, the remaining Nordic countries -- Denmark, Iceland, Sweden and Norway -- are all in the top 10. Almost every year since the United Nations General Assembly declared March 20 the International Day of Happiness in 2012, a consortium of international agencies has been issuing these happiness rankings, along with detailed reports on well-being. The rankings provide countries with a way to measure national success and develop policies that enhance well-being -- beyond economic measures like gross domestic product.

But while there may be benefits of moving past standard economic factors as markers of a country's success, the definition of happiness isn't a universal standard. "Culture can influence how people in different countries respond to surveys of happiness," says macro psychologist Kuba Krys. "We should be careful of ... making big claims based on such comparisons." Moreover, the concept of happiness, as it's currently defined and understood, may suffer from a Western bias, one common in societies that social scientists refer to as WEIRD -- Western, educated, industrialized, rich and democratic, Krys says. The rankings in the happiness report rely on responses to a single question in the Gallup World Poll: "Please imagine a ladder with steps numbered from 0 at the bottom to 10 at the top. Suppose that the top of the ladder represents the best possible life for you, and the bottom of the ladder represents the worst possible life for you. On which step of the ladder would you say you personally feel you stand at this time?" Finnish respondents, on average, stand just below the eighth rung, U.S. respondents stand roughly one rung lower, a score that lands them in 23rd place. But Krys questions whether such scores can be meaningfully compared across countries. For instance, when researchers asked 200 people in Tanzania how they selected their rung, they found that over a third, most of them with limited formal education, did not understand the question. Moreover, personality and cultural psychologist Mohsen Joshanloo notes that many people fear that admitting to a high level of happiness may cause something bad to happen. That fear can depress their scores on a standardized survey, his research shows.
