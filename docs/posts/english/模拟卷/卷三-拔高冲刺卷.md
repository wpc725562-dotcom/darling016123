*The relationship between sleep and cognitive function has been a subject of scientific inquiry for decades, but recent advances in neuroimaging have provided unprecedented insights into the mechanisms by which sleep affects the brain. What emerges from this research is a picture far more complex than simply "resting" the brain.*

*During sleep, particularly during the rapid eye movement (REM) stage, the brain engages in a process called synaptic pruning. This involves the strengthening of important neural connections while weakening those that are less frequently used. Dr. Matthew Walker, a neuroscientist at the University of California, Berkeley, describes this process as "a sort of overnight editing session" in which the brain consolidates memories and discards irrelevant information.*

*The implications for learning are profound. A study conducted at Harvard Medical School found that participants who slept for eight hours after learning a new task performed 20% better on subsequent tests compared to those who stayed awake. This improvement was attributed to the brain's ability to transfer information from the hippocampus, where short-term memories are stored, to the neocortex, where long-term memories are consolidated.*

*Furthermore, sleep deprivation has been shown to impair not only memory but also emotional regulation and decision-making abilities. *Despite this evidence, a significant portion of the population fails to meet recommended sleep guidelines. The Centers for Disease Control and Prevention (CDC) estimates that one in three American adults does not get enough sleep. This "sleep debt" accumulates over time, leading to what researchers call "cognitive fog" — a state of reduced mental clarity and processing speed that can have serious consequences in academic and professional settings.*

### Passage C

*The phenomenon of "fake news" has become a central concern in contemporary discourse, particularly in the context of social media platforms where information spreads rapidly with minimal oversight. The term refers to false or misleading information presented as news, often created with the intention of deceiving readers or advancing a particular agenda.*

*The mechanisms by which fake news spreads are rooted in human psychology. Research published in the journal *Science* demonstrated that false news stories are 70% more likely to be retweeted on Twitter than true stories. This disparity is partly explained by the novelty factor — false information is often more surprising and emotionally engaging than accurate information, making it more likely to be shared.*

*Confirmation bias plays a significant role in the consumption of fake news. Individuals tend to seek out and believe information that confirms their existing beliefs while dismissing contradictory evidence. This cognitive bias makes people particularly vulnerable to misinformation that aligns with their preconceptions, regardless of its factual accuracy.*

*Efforts to combat fake news have taken various forms. Fact-checking organizations like Snopes and PolitiFact have emerged to verify claims, and social media platforms have implemented algorithms to flag potentially misleading content. However, these approaches face significant challenges. Fact-checking is time-consuming and often reaches audiences after the misinformation has already spread. Algorithmic solutions, meanwhile, raise concerns about censorship and the suppression of legitimate speech.*

*Educational approaches may offer the most sustainable solution. Media literacy programs that teach critical thinking skills — such as evaluating source credibility, distinguishing between fact and opinion, and recognizing logical fallacies — can equip individuals with the tools they need to navigate the complex information landscape. As one researcher put it, "The best defense against misinformation is not censorship but a well-informed public capable of thinking critically about the information they encounter."*
